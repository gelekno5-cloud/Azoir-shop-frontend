How to choose pearl earrings you will actually wear.

The four pearl types, what makes one pearl better than another, and the style and care questions to settle before you buy.

Published November 2026 · Azoir & Co., New York

The short answer

Decide on the look first: a small round stud, a drop that moves, or a larger statement pearl. That choice points you to a pearl type. Then judge pearls of that type mainly by luster, the sharp, bright glow on the surface. A smaller pearl with strong luster almost always looks better than a bigger, duller one.

Last, be honest about how you will wear them. Pearls are soft, so the setting and a few care habits matter more than they do for diamonds.

Why pearl drops are everywhere this season

Pearls never really leave, but the way people wear them changes. Google's Holiday 100, its yearly list of gift ideas drawn from search trends, shows searches for "dangle pearl earrings" up 245% in 2026, as JCK reported on November 20 (JCK). The examples in that piece range from gold-plated huggies with removable freshwater pearl drops to 18K gold drops with freshwater pearls and enamel.

The appeal is easy to see. A drop catches light as you move, and a removable drop gives you two pairs of earrings in one: a plain hoop for the office, a pearl for the evening. Whatever style you choose, the questions below still apply.

The four main pearl types, compared

Most pearls sold today are cultured, meaning a pearl farmer starts the process and the mollusk does the rest. GIA groups the main cultured pearls into four types (GIA):

AkoyaSaltwater, mainly Japan and ChinaClassic white and creamRound, bright studs and matched pairs

Freshwater pearls are the most commonly produced type. They are often grown many to one mollusk and are generally sold at lower price points, which is why GIA notes their popularity with shoppers and designers (GIA). Traditional freshwater pearls also start from a small piece of mantle tissue rather than a shell bead, so they are built up from layers of nacre, the material that gives a pearl its glow (GIA).

What makes one pearl better than another

GIA judges pearls on seven value factors: size, shape, color, luster, surface quality, nacre quality and matching (GIA). For earrings, four of them do most of the work.

Luster. GIA says luster "might be the most important" of the seven. Look for a crisp reflection on the surface, as if you could almost see your face in it. Thin nacre and low luster tend to go together.

Shape. Round is the hardest shape to culture and the rarest. That makes perfect rounds the classic stud, but well-formed ovals, pears and baroque pearls can be lovely as drops, where an organic shape looks intentional.

Surface. A few small marks matter little, especially if the post or cap hides them. Many deep marks can weaken the pearl over time.

Matching. Earrings come in pairs, so the two pearls should match in size, color and luster. A mismatched pair is the most common disappointment, and the easiest to avoid by looking at them side by side.

Studs, drops or huggies

Studs are the quiet classic. Size is the main choice, and a millimeter or two changes the look more than you would expect. Measure a pair of earrings you already love with a ruler before you choose.

Drops hang below the ear on a short loop or a small cap. They suit pear and baroque shapes and show off luster as they move. Check the weight: a large pearl on a long drop pulls on the earlobe over a long evening.

Huggies with a removable drop are the flexible option. The hoop works on its own, and the pearl clips on when you want it. If you already wear small hoops every day, this is the style you are most likely to reach for.

Whatever the style, most pearl earrings hold the pearl on a small peg glued into a partly drilled hole, rather than with prongs. The metal around it is your choice. Yellow gold warms cream and golden pearls, while white metals suit cool white and dark pearls. Caring for pearl earrings

Pearl ranks only 2.5 on the Mohs hardness scale, so it scratches easily (GIA). Earrings are a kind place for pearls, because they do not knock against tables the way rings do, but a few habits help them keep their glow:

  • Put them on last, after hairspray, perfume and makeup, which GIA lists among the things that can harm pearls, along with perspiration.
  • Wipe them with a very soft, clean cloth after each wearing. For an occasional deeper clean, warm, soapy water is safe.
  • Never use an ultrasonic or steam cleaner on pearls.
  • Store them apart from diamonds and other hard stones, which can scratch them.

A word on names

Pearl names can mislead. GIA has called "freshwater akoya" a misnomer: true akoya pearls grow in a saltwater oyster, Pinctada fucata, and freshwater pearls sold under that name are not akoya pearls (GIA). Freshwater pearls can be beautiful in their own right. Just ask which mollusk and which water a pearl came from, so you know what you are comparing.

Questions

Good to know.

Which type of pearl is best for earrings?

There is no single best type. Akoya pearls are the classic choice for round white studs. Freshwater pearls come in the widest range of shapes and colors and suit drops and everyday pairs. South Sea and Tahitian pearls are the choice for larger, statement earrings in white, golden or dark tones. Start with the look you want, then compare luster within that type.

Are freshwater pearls real pearls?

Yes. Freshwater cultured pearls are real pearls, grown in mussels in lakes and ponds, mostly in China. Traditional freshwater pearls start from a small piece of mantle tissue rather than a shell bead. They differ from saltwater akoya pearls in how they are grown, not in whether they are genuine.

Can I wear pearl earrings every day?

Yes, with a little care. Pearls rank only 2.5 on the Mohs hardness scale, so put them on after perfume, hairspray and makeup, and wipe them with a soft cloth after wearing. Never put pearls in an ultrasonic or steam cleaner.

What is a freshwater akoya pearl?

It is a freshwater pearl sold under a borrowed name. True akoya pearls grow in a saltwater oyster, Pinctada fucata. GIA has called the term "freshwater akoya" a misnomer that confuses buyers. If you see it, ask which mollusk and which water the pearl came from.
